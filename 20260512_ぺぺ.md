Preprocessing

Step 1 — Tokenization
Split the raw definition string into individual words. Lowercase everything. Punctuation and
hyphens are treated as separators.

"A dog is a domesticated four-legged animal" →
["a", "dog", "is", "a", "domesticated", "four-legged", "animal"]

Step 2 — Stop word removal
Remove function words that carry no definitional meaning — articles, prepositions, conjunctions,
pronouns, auxiliaries.

["a", "dog", "is", "a", "domesticated", "four-legged", "animal"] →
["domesticated", "four-legged", "animal"]

Step 3 — Lemmatization
Reduce every word to its base form so that different conjugations of the same word map to the
same node. Why this matters: If "runs," "running," and "ran" are treated as separate nodes, the
same concept splits into three disconnected points in the graph. Lemmatization collapses them
into one.

Step 4 — Vocabulary filtering
Keep only words that have their own entry in the dictionary. If a definition uses a word with no
entry — a proper noun, abbreviation, or rare term — that word is dropped. No node, no edge.
This keeps the graph self-contained: every node in the graph can itself be looked up and defined.

Step 5 — First sense only
Each word has only one definition processed — the first one listed. "Bank" means financial
institution, not riverbank. This makes the graph tractable but loses polysemy. The paper
acknowledges this as a limitation.

Step 1：Kernel

What is the kernel?
The Kernel is the minimum vocabulary through which the dictionary defines itself. It tells you which words are part of the
dictionary's self-sustaining definitional core. The other 90% of words are peripheral — they enrich the vocabulary but the
definitional machinery does not depend on them.

Intuition
Imagine you are trying to keep only the words that matter for the definitional system.

A word that never appears in any other word's definition is useless to the network, it
consumes definitions but contributes nothing.

You can remove it without affecting anything else. Strip those words away, and some
words that previously had arrows pointing into them from the removed words may
now also be useless. Remove those too. Keep going until you cannot remove
anything else. What remains is the Kernel.

Why the Kernel is unique
No matter what order you remove words, you always end up with the same Kernel.

Removing a word with in-degree zero cannot change whether any other word has in-
degree zero except by potentially exposing new zeros. The final stable state is always
identical regardless of removal order.

Every dictionary has exactly one Kernel.  It’s about 10% of the dictionary

Step 2：Core

What is the Core?
The Core is the giant Strongly Connected Component (SCC) at the center of the Kernel. An SCC is a set of nodes where every
node is reachable from every other node by following directed edges. After all dead-end words have been stripped away, one
group remains where every word can reach every other word by following definition arrows in any direction, from any starting
point. That group is the Core. Around 75% of the Kernel and about 8% of the dictionary.

Why the Core is special
1. Self-contained. No word inside the Core needs anything from outside the Core to be defined.
2. Mutually reachable. You can navigate from any word to any other word inside it by following arrows.
3. Most abstract layer. Core words tend to be the most frequent, earliest-acquired, and most abstract words in the language —

"make," "know," "go," "say," "good," "time," "place."

What does the Core tells you?
The words inside the core sit at the definitional center of everything and corresponds precisely to the words humans learn earliest
and use most frequently.

Step 3：MinSet

What is the MinSet?
The MinSet is the smallest possible set of words that must be learned from real-world experience — not from definitions — for every other word in
the dictionary to become learnable by definition alone. Roughly 1% of the dictionary.

How it works mathematically
Every cycle needs at least one word removed to be broken. The MinSet is the smallest set of words that, if removed from the graph, eliminates every
cycle simultaneously. Once those words are removed — meaning grounded in experience — every remaining word can be reached through
definition chains that no longer loop. The entire vocabulary becomes learnable from those grounded foundations.

How are MinSets found?
Two approaches depending on dictionary size:
•

Exact — CPLEX (small dictionaries) A mathematical optimization solver. You give it the variables (one per word, 0 or 1), the objective (minimize
total words selected), and the constraints (every cycle must lose at least one word). CPLEX searches the solution space using branch and bound
— pruning combinations it can prove will never beat the current best — until it finds the provably optimal MinSet.

•

Approximate — greedy (large dictionaries) Repeatedly pick the word participating in the most remaining cycles and remove it. Not guaranteed
to be the absolute minimum but close enough, and runs in a fraction of the time. Used for Webster (~248,000 words).

Why NP-hard?
The number of cycles grows exponentially with graph size. A dictionary Kernel has potentially millions of overlapping cycles. Finding the smallest set
covering all of them simultaneously requires checking an exponential number of combinations in the worst case — no known algorithm guarantees
the exact minimum in polynomial time. Even CPLEX, with all its pruning, took days of compute time for the larger dictionaries.
